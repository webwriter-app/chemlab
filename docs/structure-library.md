# ChemLab structure library

The library supplies offline structures for the existing chemical catalog. It is separate from ChemLab's dosing and equilibrium models. Molecule Builder shows the archived structure with its source. ChemLab checks a returned structure against the catalog and the worksheet's reagent permissions before selecting a dose.

## Molecule Builder → ChemLab compatibility handoff

### Required outcome for the next implementation

Every compound selectable in Molecule Builder must be directly usable as a reagent in ChemLab, rather than being transferable only as a structural reference. This is a catalog-wide requirement covering molecular compounds and crystal references, not just organic compounds or the NaHCO₃ example. The current implementation does not yet meet this requirement across the catalog; this section is the handoff for future work, not a claim of completed coverage.

For every listed salt, provide **both a solid-salt form and an aqueous-solution form**, with explicit phase selection and concentration controls for solutions. A crystal handoff should select the solid form; an aqueous-ion overview should select its corresponding solution. Both forms must resolve to the same analytical compound and shared chemical models. Respect actual solubility: a sparingly soluble salt still needs a usable solid and a physically valid solution option, with appropriate concentration limits and undissolved excess where applicable. Do not silently substitute a solution for a selected solid, or treat a salt as fully dissolved beyond its modeled limit.

Compatibility includes dosing, chemically appropriate behaviour within the simulation's documented scope, interaction with existing reagents, and save/restore. Existing reagent permissions and structural validation remain in force. Missing identity data, recipes or chemistry models are implementation gaps to resolve for selectable catalog compounds, not a permanent reference-only exemption. Track them explicitly until the catalog-wide requirement is satisfied.

The salt half is met: all 31 salt identities carry both a solid and a stock, the [coverage report](structure-library-coverage.md) lists 62 dosed salt entries, and no reference-only identity is left; `tests/structure-coverage.test.ts` holds that state, including the absence of the block. The non-salt half is nearly met: `molecular()` builds a reagent from the graph the library already stores, so the eleven gases are dosed by volume, the four library acids and slaked lime carry their own dosing recipe, the six oxides join them through `chemlib/oxides.ts`, sodium and potassium are dosed as the water-reactive metals they are, and the 24 organic rows of the library — indicators, sugars, the polysaccharides, the fatty acids and their soaps, the plastics, the two monomers, formaldehyde and bromine water — are dosed from their own graphs as well; the catalog holds 163 of 181 dosed entries. Still open are the thirteen mixtures and reagent solutions (Petrolether, Lugol's solution, Fehling I and II, Benedict's reagent, Tollens' reagent, universal indicator, household vinegar, lemon juice, tap water, mineral water, air and sand), the five rows deliberately without a dose (paraffin oil, litmus, red cabbage juice, Schiff's reagent, olive oil; see the [coverage report](structure-library-coverage.md)), and the manual-edit pipeline, which has no implementation yet.

### Manually edited inorganic compounds

The required pipeline also covers **manually altered inorganic molecules and salts**, including edited catalog references and custom structures, using the same structure-based identification and enrichment workflow already available for organic compounds. They must be identified from their current chemical structure and, after validation and data lookup, become usable ChemLab reagents. Support must not depend on an unchanged catalog structure or an organic classification.

Editing must invalidate stale compound identity, fetched facts and dosing recipes. Re-identify the current graph or analytical salt formula, preserving complete polyatomic ions, formal charges, protonation state, stoichiometry and hydrate composition. Distinguish chemical changes from changes to drawing layout or viewing geometry. An edited reference must not inherit approval or chemical data merely from its old catalog key; failure to match the original recipe must route to fresh identification rather than automatically ending at reference-only storage.

For manually altered salts, lookup and validate **all data required for both solid and aqueous forms**: exact compound identity, phase/hydrate identity, molar mass, solid density, solubility or Ksp with units and reference conditions, and relevant acid/base, hydrolysis, complexation and other interaction data. Retain sources and resolve conflicting measurements. If the edited crystal does not establish a unique analytical formula or phase, report that specific unresolved input instead of inventing one.

Use the fetched, validated data to generate both solid and solution recipes and drive the actual solubility simulation, not only a concentration selector. Model finite solid reservoirs, dissolution, saturation, precipitation, dilution and relevant coupled equilibria consistently with existing reagents. The same salt dosed dry or in solution must reach equivalent results for equivalent final composition and conditions. Respect temperature/phase limits of the available data and document approximations. Reuse the existing carbonate and bicarbonate families rather than creating independent systems for newly identified members.

Incomplete or chemically invalid structures, ambiguous identity, missing measurements and unavailable simulation models must produce actionable reasons and remain tracked implementation gaps. Do not silently accept an arbitrary formula, guess required properties or claim correct simulation without the supporting model. Identification, validation and reagent permissions still apply to custom compounds. This extension is future implementation work; the NaHCO₃ catalog recipes below do not already implement it.

### Implemented example: NaHCO₃

Sodium bicarbonate has a dry-salt recipe under the existing `Natriumhydrogencarbonat` catalog key and a separate prepared aqueous stock under `Natriumhydrogencarbonatlösung`. Returning its unchanged archived crystal selects the dry salt through the existing structural comparison and worksheet permissions; the aqueous preview selects the stock. The solid formula unit has one ionic Na–O relationship and an explicit O–H bond. Its starting atoms are neutral because the graph loader applies the ionic charge transfer. Solvation splits the unit into Na⁺ and HCO₃⁻ and registers any excess as a solid reservoir. The CIF has no hydrogen positions and must not supply the analytical formula. The aqueous preview includes the proton and uses the conventional HCO₃⁻ label.

Both forms share the existing carbonic-acid family with `Natriumcarbonatlösung` (pKa 6.35 and 10.33). Bicarbonate starts at the intermediate protonation step with one sodium per carbon, whereas carbonate has two. Acid/base addition, CO₂ release, precipitation and saved mixtures use the shared simulation rather than a bicarbonate-specific reaction. The nominal room-temperature saturation limit is 1.14 mol/L, approximately 96 g/L divided by 84.01 g/mol; the solid-density parameter is 2.16 g/mL. Sources: [Solvay product safety summary](https://www.solvay.com/sites/g/files/srpend221/files/2021-01/PSS-Sodium-Bicarbonate.pdf) and [Sigma-Aldrich sodium bicarbonate](https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/US/en/product/sigald/s6014). The existing ideal-concentration and gas-saturation approximations still apply; this does not add thermal decomposition or atmospheric CO₂ exchange.

### Implemented example: potassium halides and nitrate

Four further salt identities now carry both forms: `Kaliumchlorid` with `Kaliumchloridlösung`, `Kaliumbromid` with `Kaliumbromidlösung`, `Kaliumiodid` with `Kaliumiodidlösung`, and `Kaliumnitrat` with `Kaliumnitratlösung`. The aqueous stock takes its concentration limit from the tabulated saturation in `chemlib/saltSolubility.ts` (4.02, 4.57, 6.09 and 2.77 mol/L), the dry salt carries the tabulated solid density (1.98, 2.75, 3.12 and 2.11 g/mL), and both resolve to the same analytical compound: a dosed solid dissolves, settles at the same saturation as the stock and redissolves on dilution. The three halides reuse the existing chloride ion; bromide, iodide and potassium are new ion definitions, and every fragment matches its table SMILES exactly (`[Br-]`, `[I-]`, and `[O-][N+](=O)[O-]` for nitrate).

Potassium nitrate is the first catalog solid whose polyatomic ion needs formal charges inside one formula unit. `Molecule.addBond` transfers charge along the ionic K–O bond by electronegativity, so the stored graph pre-compensates that transfer with `charges: [0, 1, 0, 0, -1]` instead of a naive K⁺/NO₃⁻ pair: the loaded formula unit comes out as `[K+].[O-][N+]([O-])=O`, the nitrate form of the table, and the dry salt dissolves into the same ions as the stock. The dry salts stand together in one solid block ahead of the stocks in `data/chemicals.ts`; the coverage report joins a pair by the catalog formula, not by position. Naming follows the bicarbonate pair: the stock keeps a distinct catalog key and a display name without the solution suffix, so the dry salt and the solution do not read as duplicates.

`tests/salt-forms.test.ts` covers all four pairs: catalog phase and concentration limit against the tabulated saturation, complete dissolution of 0.01 mol in 100 mL to 0.1 mol/L of each ion at pH 7, agreement with the same amount of stock, a saturated reservoir that conserves moles, and redissolution on dilution.

### Implemented example: sodium bromide and iodide

`Natriumbromid` and `Natriumiodid` were the last two solids without a stock, because neither salt was tabulated and a stock would have had no saturation limit. Both now carry a `saltSolubility` row, derived the same way as the other freely soluble salts: the saturated mass fraction converted with the density of that same solution (90.5 g per 100 g water as 47.5 % and 1.53 g/mL give 7.06 mol/L for NaBr, 184 g per 100 g water as 64.8 % and 1.84 g/mL give 7.95 mol/L for NaI). `Natriumbromidlösung` and `Natriumiodidlösung` take that limit, and the dry salts read their solid density from the same row. The table is now the single source for every dosed salt: `salt()` in `data/chemicals.ts` looks its entry up by catalog formula instead of taking a density argument, so a formula unit and its solid density can no longer disagree. `tests/salt-forms.test.ts` runs both new pairs through dissolution, pH 7, agreement with the stock and the saturated reservoir.

### Implemented example: ammonium chloride and sodium acetate

`Ammoniumchlorid` and `Natriumacetat` were reference-only entries with a builder crystal but no dose at all. Both are freely soluble and needed a `saltSolubility` row before they could carry either form, derived like the alkali halides from the saturated mass fraction and the density of that same solution: 29.7 g NH₄Cl per 100 g water at 25 °C is 22.9 % with 1.077 g/mL, which gives 4.6 mol/L, and 46.4 g sodium acetate per 100 g water at 20 °C is 31.7 % with 1.11 g/mL, which gives 4.29 mol/L. The dry salts take their bulk densities from the same row (1.527 and 1.528 g/mL), `Ammoniumchloridlösung` and `Natriumacetatlösung` their concentration limits, and the structural preview stays with the existing catalog keys.

These are the first dosed salts whose own ions are the weak electrolytes rather than a hydrolysing cation from the hydroxide table. Ammonium joins the existing ammonia family (pKa 9.25) and acetate the existing acetic-acid family (pKa 4.75), so the pH comes out of the shared speciation without a new equilibrium: 0.1 mol/L ammonium chloride reads pH 5.13 and sodium acetate pH 8.88, from the dry salt exactly as from the stock. Above saturation each salt keeps its reservoir at the tabulated limit, conserves both ions and redissolves on dilution, which exercises the same `registerSolidSalt` path as the other pairs. Neutralisation also runs through the shared model: sodium acetate with hydrochloric acid buffers at 4.75, ammonium chloride with sodium hydroxide at 9.25.

The table has to spell its ions the way Kekulé writes them. Ammonium is `[N+]` in `chemlib/saltSolubility.ts`, and `[NH4+]` is only an alias of the acid family; with the alias in the row, both `registerSolidSalt` and the settling loop miss, the salt dissolves completely instead of settling, and a saturated charge reads 6.6 mol/L NH₄⁺ at pH 4.2 with no solid at all. `tests/salt-ammonium-acetate.test.ts` therefore compares the fragment list against the table before it doses anything, and then covers the catalog phase and density, dissolution stoichiometry, stock parity, the two documented pH values, the saturated reservoir with its dilution step and the two neutralisations.

### Implemented example: salts with more than one ion of a kind

Seven further salt identities now carry a dry solid and a stock: `Silbernitrat` with `Silbernitratlösung`, `Bariumchlorid` with `Bariumchloridlösung`, `Calciumchlorid` with `Calciumchloridlösung`, `Eisen(III)-chlorid` with `Eisen(III)-chloridlösung`, `Natriumcarbonat` with `Natriumcarbonatlösung`, `Natriumsulfat` with `Natriumsulfatlösung`, and `Zinksulfat` with `Zinksulfatlösung`. Every pair already had its stock and its tabulated saturation and solid density in `chemlib/saltSolubility.ts` (8.80, 1.38, 5.04, 2.69, 2.01, 1.30 and 1.79 mol/L; 4.35, 3.86, 2.15, 2.90, 2.54, 2.66 and 3.54 g/mL), so the missing half was the formula unit of the solid. `Calciumchlorid` had been a reference-only entry until now and becomes the dosed solid.

These are the first catalog solids whose formula unit carries more than one ion of a kind, which changes how the graph is written. `Molecule.addBond` moves exactly one electron per ionic bond toward the more electronegative atom, so a salt needs one ionic bond per anion: `BaCl₂`, `CaCl₂` and `FeCl₃` give their two or three chlorides one electron each and serialise as `[Ba2+]`, `[Ca2+]` and `[Fe3+]` next to the matching number of `[Cl-]`, exactly the entries of the table. The oxyanion salts take the other route: the sulfate and carbonate skeletons stay neutral and the two alkali metals or the zinc atom transfer one electron per bond to the two unprotonated oxygens, so the fragment comes out as `[O-]S(=O)(=O)[O-]` or `[O-]C(=O)[O-]` and `[Na+] ×2` or `[Zn2+]`. `Silbernitrat` reuses the pre-computed `charges` of `Kaliumnitrat`, because four-bonded nitrogen cannot be neutral in the first place. A wrong bond count is silent: `registerSolidSalt` falls through to `sDissolveSalt`, so the dry salt would dissolve completely and never hold a reservoir, which is why `tests/salt-solids.test.ts` compares the fragment list against the table before it doses anything.

The chemistry stays inside the existing equilibrium models. The hydrolysing cations carry the pH of their stocks: 0.1 mol/L `Eisen(III)-chlorid` reads pH 1.64, `Zinksulfat` 5.52 and `Silbernitrat` 6.48 from the silver hydrolysis family, while sodium carbonate reads 11.66 as a base and sodium sulfate 7.52 from the hydrogen sulfate step; `Calciumchlorid` and `Bariumchlorid` stay close to neutral at pH 6.73 and 6.93. Dosing 0.1 mol of the dry salt into 100 mL water therefore yields the same pH, the same stored ions and the same volume as the corresponding stock. Above saturation the solid keeps a reservoir at the tabulated limit and redissolves on dilution; `Eisen(III)-chlorid` settles at an analytical 2.72 mol/L instead of 2.69, because the solubility product reads the free Fe³⁺ that hydrolysis lowers. The new solids join the existing interactions unchanged: `Silbernitrat` shows the chloride with `Natriumchloridlösung` as AgCl, `Bariumchlorid` precipitates BaSO₄ from `Natriumsulfatlösung`, and `Natriumcarbonat` releases the same CO₂ as its stock when dosed into hydrochloric acid.

`tests/salt-solids.test.ts` covers all seven pairs: catalog phase, density and concentration limit against the table, the fragment list against the table ions, complete dissolution to the right stoichiometry at 0.1 mol/L, agreement with the same amount of stock in pH and ion amounts, the documented pH of the five solids that move the water equilibrium, a saturated reservoir that conserves both ions and redistributes on dilution, and the three interaction cases above. None of the seven solids has a builder structure yet, so they extend ChemLab's solid selection ahead of the structures library. The builder catalog lists only entries that resolve to a structure or to the constituents of a mixture, so these salts do not appear there as rows that cannot load, and the [coverage report](structure-library-coverage.md) keeps them in its "entries without a builder structure" list until a crystal or molecule definition exists for them.

### Implemented example: sparingly soluble salts

The three remaining salts without any dose were the insoluble ones: `Silberchlorid`, `Bariumsulfat` and `Calciumcarbonat` had builder structures and `saltSolubility` rows with a Ksp, but a stock needs a concentration limit and a saturated solution of a Ksp-only salt has no tabulated saturation. Each row now carries a second number next to its `ksp`, `saturation`, and the three pairs `Silberchlorid`/`Silberchloridlösung (gesättigt)`, `Bariumsulfat`/`Bariumsulfatlösung (gesättigt)` and `Calciumcarbonat`/`Calciumcarbonatlösung (gesättigt)` take the dry solid density and the stock limit from that row.

The new field is deliberately *not* the equilibrium. It is the ideal solubility from the Ksp, rounded down to two significant digits so the stock can never sit above its own solubility product: 1.34·10⁻⁵ → 1.3·10⁻⁵ mol/L for AgCl, 1.05·10⁻⁵ → 1.0·10⁻⁵ for BaSO₄ and 5.83·10⁻⁵ → 5.8·10⁻⁵ for CaCO₃. `saltEquilibria` keeps reading `data.ksp`, so a saturated stock dosed at its own limit is still clear and still below the product, while a dry charge above the limit settles on the root of the Ksp — 1.3416·10⁻⁵ mol/L Ag⁺ and 1.0488·10⁻⁵ mol/L Ba²⁺, both measurably above the rounded stock limit. Calcium carbonate dissolves to a free 1.11495·10⁻⁴ mol/L Ca²⁺ at pH 9.907, because the carbonate anion is the base of the carbonic-acid family and only a fraction of the dissolved amount is free.

The formula units follow the existing recipes: `silverChloride` is the plain 1:1 case of `salt()`, while `bariumSulfate` and `calciumCarbonate` copy the ionic bond pattern of `zincSulfate` and `sodiumCarbonate` with the alkaline-earth atom in place of the soluble one. `solutionDisplayNames` maps the three stocks back to the solid's name, so the pair reads like the bicarbonate and potassium pairs instead of repeating the formula. All three solids were removed from the reference-only block, which at the time left ammonium sulfate, potassium thiocyanate, potassium permanganate, potassium sodium tartrate, iron(II) sulfide and the hydrates as the documented gaps; every one of them carries a dose now.

The interesting cases are interactions rather than dissolution, because the existing equilibria already carry them. A soluble chloride pushes the saturated stock's own dissolved silver back into the solid (1.28 µmol AgCl from 1 mL 0.1 mol/L hydrochloric acid, free 1.8177·10⁻⁷ mol/L Ag⁺, pH 3.004), sodium sulfate the barium (0.99 µmol BaSO₄), sodium carbonate the dry calcium carbonate completely with a free 7.8364·10⁻⁸ mol/L Ca²⁺, and ammonia dissolves silver chloride through the existing silver ammine complexes (0.01 mol/L Ag⁺ at pH 11.464). Hydrochloric acid dissolves calcium carbonate and releases 6.6 mmol CO₂, while barium sulfate keeps its reservoir under the same acid — the sulfate proof the electrochemistry interactions already rely on. `tests/salt-sparing.test.ts` covers the catalog forms and the rounded limit against the ideal solubility, complete dissolution below the product, the reservoir at the Ksp that stays above the stock's limit, stock/dry parity at 10 µmol/L and at the stock's own limit, and the five interactions above.

### Implemented example: ammonium sulfate, thiocyanate and permanganate

The last three freely soluble salts were reference-only entries with a builder crystal but no dose: `Ammoniumsulfat`, `Kaliumthiocyanat` and `Kaliumpermanganat`. Each now carries a `saltSolubility` row, derived like the alkali halides from the saturated mass fraction and the density of that same solution, so the dry salt and its stock share one limit. Ammonium sulfate dissolves 4.1 mol/L at 25 °C (3.9 mol/L at 0 °C) and its solid is 1.769 g/mL ([solubility compilation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4817497/)). Potassium thiocyanate dissolves 217 g per 100 mL water at 20 °C, which is 68.5 wt %; with 1.40 g/mL estimated for the saturated solution that is 9.8 mol/L, and the solid is 1.886 g/mL ([ChemicalBook](https://www.chemicalbook.com/ChemicalProductProperty_EN_CB6853074.htm)). Potassium permanganate dissolves 6.38 g per 100 mL at 20 °C; with 1.04 g/mL estimated for the saturated solution that is 0.39 mol/L, and the solid is 2.703 g/mL ([solubility table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solubility_table)). Both densities of the concentrated stocks are estimates rather than measurements, and the two limits carry that approximation.

`Ammoniumsulfat` is the first formula unit with two cations of one kind next to a doubly charged anion, so the ionic pattern of `CaCl₂` appears twice: each ammonium hands its electron to one of the two unprotonated sulfate oxygens, which leaves two `[N+]` with four explicit hydrogens each, the neutral `SO₄` skeleton and the table's `[O-]S(=O)(=O)[O-]`. `Kaliumthiocyanat` keeps the carbon–nitrogen triple bond and puts the charge on sulfur, so it serialises as the `N#C[S-]` of the table rather than the readable `[S-]C#N`. `Kaliumpermanganat` reuses the `oxoanion` helper with three Mn=O double bonds and one Mn–O⁻, which gives `[O-][Mn](=O)(=O)=O` and one ionic K–O bond; no other entry writes a manganese oxyanion, so its fragment list is the only place that charge is written down.

Ammonium sulfate is the only dosed solid whose cation and anion are both weak electrolytes of existing families. 0.1 mol/L of the salt is 0.2 mol/L ammonium next to 0.1 mol/L sulfate, and the bath reads pH 5.491. The same 0.2 mol/L as ammonium chloride reads 4.974, because the second step of the sulfuric-acid family takes part of the released protons back as hydrogen sulfate; the difference is that coupling, not a new reaction. Two millimoles of sodium hydroxide in 120 mL shift the same bath to pH 8.296 through the ammonia family. The sulfate takes part in the precipitation equilibria as well: 1 mL of 0.1 mol/L barium chloride in the stock leaves 0.1 mmol BaSO₄, a free 1.12·10⁻⁹ mol/L Ba²⁺ and pH 5.489.

Thiocyanate and permanganate are the first dosed ions that no equilibrium table contains. Their salts dissolve to the full stoichiometry and leave the water equilibrium untouched at exactly pH 7, because neither ion reaches a protonation, hydrolysis or complex step. That is a deliberate limitation rather than an identity problem: the iron(III) thiocyanate complexes behind the blood-red proof and the reduction of permanganate in acid are not modelled, so `Kaliumthiocyanat` and `Kaliumpermanganat` act as colourless spectator salts. The solubility rows note both omissions, and the coverage report keeps the two pairs paired rather than reference-only.

Above saturation the three salts hold a reservoir at the tabulated limit, conserve their ions and redissolve when ten times the water is added, and the stock dosed at its own limit stays clear. `tests/salt-freely-soluble.test.ts` covers the catalog forms against the table, dry/stock parity at 0.1 mol/L, the two spectator baths at pH 7, the ammonium sulfate pH pair and its neutralisation, the barium sulfate precipitation, the reservoir with its dilution step and the clear stock at its limit.

### Implemented example: copper sulfate pentahydrate

`Kupfer(II)-sulfat-Pentahydrat` was the last stock without a solid whose own chemistry was already complete: `CuSO4` carried a stock (`Kupfer(II)-sulfatlösung`, 1.29 mol/L saturation), a `3.6` g/mL solid density for the anhydrous powder and a crystal reference in the builder, so the missing half was the dose. The catalog entry is now the solid, at the density of the blue crystals, 2.286 g/mL measured for chalcanthite ([Handbook of Mineralogy](https://www.handbookofmineralogy.org/pdfs/chalcanthite.pdf), [Mindat](https://www.mindat.org/min-19816.html)). The structure is the anhydrous sulfate skeleton of `Zinksulfat` plus five neutral water fragments, each with one `O–H` pair, so the formula unit holds 21 atoms and 16 bonds. The crystal hydrogens hang on their own oxygen and are never bonded to the sulfate: the water is a fragment of its own, exactly as `crystalIons` reads a hydrate when it groups the crystal view.

A hydrate cannot be dosed like an anhydrous salt, because the formula unit carries five waters that leave the crystal and join the solvent, and because a mole of crystals occupies more volume than a mole of the anhydrous powder. Both facts are now part of the solid path. `registerSolidSalt` looks for neutral water in the formula unit and books `amount × count` of it through `addMolecule`, so a dry 0.01 mol dose into 100 mL leaves the ions in 100.9 mL: the bath reads marginally below 0.1 mol/L because the crystal water is now part of the solvent, and it matches the corresponding stock at that concentration in pH and ion amounts. The anhydrous reservoir keeps the tabulated bulk, so an overdosed 0.2 mol charge settles 0.04433 L/mol, the bulk of the anhydrous powder, and not the 0.10922 L/mol the crystals had taken.

The dosed bulk volume therefore moved from the solubility row to the molecule. `Molecule` carries an optional `solidBulkLitresPerMol`, `data/measuredDose.ts` fills it from the recipe's own molar mass and density, and `registerSolidSalt` swaps the tabulated anhydrous bulk for it before the reservoir is booked; `sDissolveSalt` releases the same number for salts that never settle. A saved dose keeps the field as `bulk` on its constituent, so a recalled hydrate still occupies the volume of its crystals; older recipes without it fall back to the tabulated anhydrous bulk, which is what an unhydrated entry wants anyway.

Above saturation the pentahydrate behaves like its stock and adds one case the anhydrous salts do not show: a saturated copper bath sits just above its own hydroxide product, so a trace settles as `Cu(OH)₂` next to the sulfate reservoir. `tests/salt-hydrate.test.ts` covers the formula unit and the density, complete dissolution with the crystal water in the solvent volume, the reservoir with the anhydrous bulk and the hydroxide trace, and the saved recipe with and without the bulk field. The coverage report counts the pair as paired, and `tests/structure-coverage.test.ts` treats a dosed hydrate as `paired` once the catalog formula without its crystal water finds the stock of the same salt: the pentahydrate joined the copper stock here, and the calcium and magnesium sulfates followed in the next example.

### Implemented example: the remaining sulfate hydrates

The last three sulfate references were decided hydrates: `Eisen(II)-sulfat-Heptahydrat` already had its stock, while `Calciumsulfat-Dihydrat` and `Magnesiumsulfat-Heptahydrat` had a builder crystal and nothing else. All three now carry both forms. `sulfateHydrate` in `data/chemicals.ts` builds each formula unit like the pentahydrate: the zinc sulfate bond pattern, then one unbonded `H₂O` fragment per crystal water, three atoms and two bonds each ahead of the sulfate skeleton. The dose density is the density of the mineral — melanterite 1.895 g/mL ([Mindat](https://www.mindat.org/min-16198.html)), gypsum 2.32 g/mL ([Mindat](https://www.mindat.org/min-1784.html)) and epsomite 1.68 g/mL ([Mindat](https://www.mindat.org/min-1393.html)) beside [chalcanthite](https://www.mindat.org/min-19816.html) from the pentahydrate example. No new simulation machinery was needed: `registerSolidSalt` already separates the ions of the formula unit and hands one water per fragment to the solvent, and `Molecule.solidBulkLitresPerMol` already carries the volume of the crystals, so a dry heptahydrate behaves exactly like the pentahydrate.

`Magnesiumsulfat-Heptahydrat` was the only one of the three whose salt had no `saltSolubility` row, because nothing in the catalog dosed magnesium before. The row follows the pattern of the freely soluble salts: 35.1 g MgSO₄ per 100 g water at 20 °C are 0.2916 mol in a 135.1 g solution, that is about 26.0 % by mass; with roughly 1.295 g/mL for that solution they fill 104.3 mL, which gives 2.80 mol/L ([ChemicalBook](https://www.chemicalbook.com/ChemicalProductProperty_EN_CB9122608.htm)). The heptahydrate's own 71 g per 100 mL at 20 °C cross-check to 2.88 mol/L, the anhydrous powder is 2.66 g/mL, and `Magnesiumsulfatlösung` takes the 2.80 mol/L limit.

Gypsum needed the other kind of row. `CaSO4` was already tabulated with a `ksp` of 4.9·10⁻⁵ but without a stock limit, because a solution of a sparingly soluble salt has no tabulated saturation. The row now carries `saturation: 6.9·10⁻³`, the ideal solubility √(4.9·10⁻⁵) = 7.00·10⁻³ mol/L rounded down, so `Calciumsulfatlösung (gesättigt)` stays below its own product like the silver chloride, barium sulfate and calcium carbonate pairs. Gypsum really dissolves about 0.014 mol/L, twice the ideal value, because the model has no `CaSO₄(aq)` ion pair, the same reason the other three read low. The limit therefore only caps the concentration selector, while a dry charge settles on the root of the product: 7.00·10⁻³ mol/L free Ca²⁺, which sits *above* the stock's rounded limit. That is why `tests/salt-hydrate.test.ts` compares a dissolved amount with the `ksp` wherever one is tabulated and with `saturation` only where none is.

The crystals and the anhydrous powder of the same salt occupy different volumes, and every hydrate carries its own:

| Hydrate | Crystal L/mol | Anhydrous salt L/mol |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| CuSO₄·5 H₂O | 0.10922 | 0.04433 |
| FeSO₄·7 H₂O | 0.14671 | 0.04173 |
| CaSO₄·2 H₂O | 0.07421 | 0.04584 |
| MgSO₄·7 H₂O | 0.14671 | 0.04525 |

The two heptahydrates land on the same volume by coincidence: the iron salt carries the heavier anhydrous part and the denser crystal, so 278.01 g/mol over 1.895 g/mL and 246.47 g/mol over 1.68 g/mL both give 146.7 mL per mole. A dissolved dose returns its crystal volume, a settled reservoir reads the tabulated anhydrous bulk of its own row, and a saved mixture keeps both through `bulk` on the constituent.

`tests/salt-hydrate.test.ts` runs table-driven over all four sulfates, so the pentahydrate and the three new hydrates share one set of five checks: the formula unit with `6 + 3·waters` atoms, `6 + 2·waters` bonds and a crystal density below the anhydrous one of the table; a dry dose that dissolves completely, puts its crystal water into the solvent volume and matches a stock at the same concentration in pH and ion amounts; an overdose that keeps an anhydrous reservoir, settles on the root of its product, closes its mass balance and books the anhydrous bulk; the saved recipe with and without its `bulk` field; and each stock's limit at its own `saturation` and below its product. `tests/structure-coverage.test.ts` now reads `CaSO₄`, `MgSO₄`, `Na₂S₂O₃` and `KNaC₄H₄O₆` as paired.

### Implemented example: sodium thiosulfate and potassium sodium tartrate

The last two reference-only salts were hydrates whose chemistry the model did not carry. `Natriumthiosulfat-Pentahydrat` needed a thiosulfate ion, and `Kaliumnatriumtartrat` needed a tartrate that runs through the existing tartaric-acid family.

**Thiosulfate.** `[O-]S(=O)([S-])=O` is the anion of a strong acid, so it gets no `acidbase` entry and travels as a spectator like the thiocyanate and the permanganate: a 0.01 mol/L stock reads pH 7.0000 and presents only H₃O⁺ and OH⁻. The row is the usual 2:1 sodium salt. 70.1 g of the anhydrous salt per 100 g water at 20 °C are 0.443 mol in 170.1 g of solution, which fills about 128 mL at 1.33 g/mL for the saturated solution, so `saturation: 3.6` ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_thiosulfate)). The anhydrous salt measures 2.345 g/mL and the pentahydrate 1.667 g/mL. The catalog solid is the pentahydrate (M 248.172): a dry dose releases its five crystal waters into the solvent, carries the crystal density as its dose bulk and afterwards behaves like the stock.

**Tartrate.** Tartaric acid was already in the acid-base table under `O=C(O)C(O)C(O)C(O)=O` with its four `C₄H` stages. `KNaC4H4O6` is the first *double salt* of the catalog: its row repeats the ion pattern with two different cations, one potassium and one sodium per tartrate. `sSaltIons` and `saltFragments` already count per ion of the formula unit, so the simulation needed no new code. 630 g of the tetrahydrate per litre of water at 20 °C are 2.23 mol/L ([SCBT](https://www.scbt.com/p/potassium-sodium-tartrate-6381-59-5)), taken as `saturation: 2.2`. Only the crystal density 1.79 g/mL is tabulated and it stands in for the anhydrous reservoir, which merely sets the bulk volume of a sediment.

A tartrate stock is weakly basic: 0.01 mol in 100 mL reads pH 8.6690 with one K⁺, one Na⁺ and the whole tartrate amount in the anion stage. Half-neutralising the salt moves the bath to (2.98 + 4.34)/2 = 3.666, the same relation the other diprotic families follow, and all three intervening `C₄H` stages appear beside it. That is why the tartrate needed a real `acidbase` entry instead of the spectator treatment the thiosulfate gets.

`tests/salt-thiosulfate-tartrate.test.ts` checks both rows against `saltIonList` and against the catalog forms, the fragments of a formula unit with their crystal water, dry/stock parity at 0.1 mol/L, the tartrate pH with its fraction and its half-neutralised point, the neutral thiosulfate bath, an overdose that keeps its reservoir at `saturation` and dilution, and the saved `bulk` of both hydrates. `tests/structure-coverage.test.ts` reads both identities as paired.

### Implemented example: iron(II) sulfide

`Eisen(II)-sulfid` was the last reference-only salt. It needed no new equilibrium: the constants were known and the dry solid already settled correctly, but the settling loop carried a frozen protonation share from pass to pass, and acid poured onto the settled solid stalled at a fraction of the salt — the very reaction the salt is dosed for. Both halves are implemented now.

The constants are Ksp 6 · 10⁻¹⁹ ([URI solubility table](https://www.chm.uri.edu/weuler/chmi112/refmater/KspTable.html)) and 4.84 g/mL for troilite ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron(II)_sulfide)). `saltSolubility.FeS` carries them with a `saturation` of 3.6 · 10⁻⁷ mol/L, and the pair `Eisen(II)-sulfid`/`Eisen(II)-sulfidlösung (gesättigt)` takes the solid density and the stock limit from that row. Unlike the four salts whose stock limit is rounded down from the root of the product (silver chloride, barium sulfate, calcium carbonate, gypsum), that saturation lies *above* the root by 470 times, and the difference is the anion, not the rule: the dissolved analytical amount of a hydrolysing ion is not its free concentration. At the bath's own pH of 7.651 the sulfide sits almost entirely as HS⁻ and H₂S (pKa 7.0 and 12.9), only 4.6 · 10⁻⁶ of the family is free S²⁻, and the free-ion product lands on the Ksp. The round 3.6 · 10⁻⁷ is the dissolved amount the settling loop measures, rounded down so a stock dosed at its own limit stays clear and below its product.

The fix is in the loop. `Simulation.saltProjection` re-solves the acid-base speciation for every trial amount: `SaltIon.concentrationAt(delta, state)` returns the free share at the proton concentration the trial state produces, and the unbound pools and the spectator charge keep the complex and charge balances consistent with it. Because the projection moves the family's analytical total and its protonation together, the bracket inside `equilibrateSalts` is monotone again and the sixty-fourth-pass stall is gone; a settled sulfide now dissolves in one settling sequence and keeps its reservoir while it is saturated. The stall was a spurious fixed point, not a missing equilibrium: while each pass extrapolated linearly at the protonation fraction of the previous pH, the alternating passes moved between 8.33 · 10⁻² mol/L at pH 4.04 and 6.31 · 10⁻⁴ mol/L at pH 0.781, twelve decades apart in the sulfide share.

Measured on the implemented pair:

- 0.01 mol in 100 mL water comes to rest at 3.636 · 10⁻⁷ mol/L dissolved, pH 7.651, reservoir 10.0000 mmol and no gas; twice the charge leaves the same solution behind.
- 0.01 mol in 120 mL of 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid dissolves completely to 8.333 · 10⁻² mol/L of each ion, pH 0.079, which is exactly the 0.02 mol of protons that `FeS + 2 HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂S` consumes.
- Acid onto the settled solid now reacts: 0.01 mol in 100 mL water followed by 100 mL of 1 mol/L acid dissolves completely to 0.05 mol/L at pH 0.398 and gives the released bulk volume back to the liquid. The dose order does not change the bath.
- Less acid leaves no room for the gas: 0.01 mol in 50 mL of 1 mol/L acid reaches 0.2 mol/L Fe²⁺ and 0.1 mol/L sulfide, the Henry limit of hydrogen sulfide, and 5 mmol leave the vessel as H₂S.
- A stock dosed above its limit precipitates instead of staying supersaturated: 100 mL of a 10 µmol/L stock falls to 3.636 · 10⁻⁷ mol/L and deposits the difference, the same bath the dry salt reaches.

The row has to spell the sulfide `[S2-]`, the SMILES `saltFragments` builds for the S²⁻ ion; the acid-base family carries `[S-2]` and `[S2-]` as aliases, but `registerSolidSalt` matches the fragment literally and a row keyed on `[S-2]` registers no reservoir at all (measured: the whole dose dissociates, ion product 1.9 · 10⁻⁸). `tests/salt-iron-sulfide.test.ts` compares the formula unit's fragments against the table before it doses anything, then covers the catalog forms, the saturation rule, the saturated bath with its reservoir invariance, both acid orders with and without the escaping gas, the stock's parity with the dry salt, the supersaturated stock and save/restore with and without the saved `bulk`.

Every salt in the catalog now carries a solid and a stock. The [coverage report](structure-library-coverage.md) lists the 62 dosed salt entries and all 31 identities paired, with no reference-only identity left.

### Implemented example: reagents from the stored structure

`data/structures/catalog.generated.json` already holds a reviewed molecular graph for every molecule the library lists, so a selectable compound does not need a second, hand-drawn definition before it can be dosed. `scripts/generate-structure-definitions.ts` converts the molecule entries into the flat `MoleculeDefinition` format and writes `data/structureDefinitions.generated.ts`; `molecular(name, category, formula, density, phase, limit)` in `data/chemicals.ts` builds a catalog entry from one of those graphs.

- The conversion maps atomic numbers to the lowercase English element names of `chemlib/ElementData.ts`, turns the 1-based library atom ids into the 0-based `from-to:type:order` bond strings of `data/moleculeDefinition.ts`, and keeps the per-atom formal charges. Crystal entries are skipped: a lattice holds many formula units, while a dosed salt is one formula unit from `chemlib/saltSolubility.ts`.
- Only spectator substances take this path. An acid, a base or a salt keeps a hand-written definition, because the dissociation of the model hangs on `acidbase` and `ionic` bonds and on the ion spellings of the solubility table. A compound the library does not carry keeps its entry without a recipe, so the palette still lists it and the coverage report keeps counting the gap.
- The generator is bundled and run from the repository root like the coverage generator (`node_modules/.bin/esbuild scripts/generate-structure-definitions.ts --bundle --platform=node --format=esm --outfile=.structure-definitions-bundle.mjs && node .structure-definitions-bundle.mjs`, `--check` verifies the tracked file). The conversion itself lives in `scripts/structure-definition-model.ts`, so a test and the generator cannot disagree.
- `tests/structure-definitions.test.ts` re-runs the generator against the tracked module, checks that every molecule entry round-trips into the same atoms, the same number of single and double bonds, the same total charge and a SMILES, and checks that each `molecular()` entry doses exactly the generated graph instead of falling back to a recipe-less entry.
- The first seven reagents are benzaldehyde, bromoethane, dichloromethane, isoamyl acetate, myristyl myristate, tetradecan-1-ol and 3-methylbutan-1-ol. They dissolve, dilute and accompany reactions as neutral molecules, which is what the model already does for the hand-written organics.

### Implemented example: gases dosed by volume

The eleven catalog gases (`Wasserstoff`, `Sauerstoff`, `Stickstoff`, `Kohlenstoffdioxid`, `Chlor`, `Methan`, `Ethen`, `Propan`, `Butan`, `Ethin`, `Chlorethen (Vinylchlorid)`) are the first reagents that are dosed as the volume they take up rather than as a portion. `gasReagent()` in `data/chemicals.ts` builds their entry from the library graph, keeps a hand-written `MoleculeDefinition` as the fallback for a name the library does not carry, and writes a recipe with `phase: "gas"`, the id of the gas in `chemlib/gasEvolution.ts` and a density of molar mass per molar volume (0.08 g/L for CO₂) so the palette card can still show a mass. `prepareDose()` branches on that phase into `prepareGasDose()`, which turns the volume into moles with the molar volume of 24.47 L/mol and adds neither a liquid nor a bulk volume: a gas dose takes no room in the vessel and cannot overflow it.

What happens next depends on the gas, and the split is the difference between a spectator gas and one the aqueous model already knows:

- **Carbon dioxide** enters the solution as the carbonic-acid family. The model never stores dissolved CO₂ as a linear molecule: `structureManager` keeps it as `C(=O)(O)O` with pKa 6.35 and 10.33, which is the same family that `sGasEvolution` removes the excess from. The dose therefore books carbonic acid and the ordinary gas evolution trims it back to the tabulated 0.034 mol/L at 25 °C and 1 bar. 100 mL of gas in 100 mL of water leaves 3.41 mmol (0.034 mol/L) dissolved, 0.67 mmol escaped and pH 3.909, the value the carbonate/hydrochloric-acid route also reports.
- **Every other gas** is offered to the vessel through `Mixture.offeredGas` and taken up by `Mixture.admitGas()`: the solution dissolves up to its Henry saturation and the rest passes into `evolvedGas`, which is the gas space and the quantity a collection tube reads. A dose of 100 mL is 4.087 mmol, so oxygen keeps 0.130 mmol dissolved (1.3 mmol/L) and sends 3.96 mmol to the gas space, nitrogen 0.064 and 4.02 mmol, ethin 4.0 mmol and 0.087 mmol. The palette gases needed seven new rows in `evolvedGases` for this (N₂ 0.64, CH₄ 1.4, C₂H₄ 4.7, C₂H₂ 40, C₃H₈ 1.5, C₄H₁₀ 1.2, C₂H₃Cl 43 mmol/L); hydrogen, oxygen, chlorine and carbon dioxide were already tabulated, the last four also because an electrode needs them.
- **Chlorine** needs no route of its own but shows the coupled equilibrium: 4.09 mmol in 100 mL of water dissolve completely (the saturation is 90 mmol/L), then half of it hydrolyses to hypochlorous acid, chloride and a proton, so the vessel holds 2.05 mmol HOCl and 2.04 mmol Cl₂ at pH 1.689, which is what the acid-base solver reads from `CHLORINE_HYDROLYSIS` without any gas-specific code.

Gas saturation is a property of the solution, not of the dose, so the same dose behaves differently in different vessels: the same 100 mL of chlorine in 1 L of water hydrolyses further and stays clear of the saturation, while a dose into a dry beaker keeps everything in the gas space. A gas dosed into a vessel without liquid stays there and is **not** re-dissolved when water is added later. That is the documented treatment of an escaped educt (EC-12): the model separates the dissolved amount from the headspace, and only the dissolved part is available to a reaction. Re-absorbing on a later addition would need a mass-transfer term between headspace and solution, which the gas model does not have.

The palette treats a gas like any other reagent, with three differences: the amount menu offers 100 mL by default and at most 500 mL (`GAS_DOSE_MAX_ML`, 20.4 mmol), one dose step is 5 mL instead of 1 mL (`GAS_DOSE_STEP_ML`) so a held dose shows a stream in reasonable time, and `doseHint()` returns no hint for a gas. A gas neither falls in as grains nor pours, so the vessel animation is left to the bubbles that `VesselView` already spawns whenever `evolvedGasMol` grows. Saved doses keep the recipe and the volume, so the amount survives save/restore; the offer itself belongs to the dose and is consumed by the first vessel that receives it.

`tests/gas-reagents.test.ts` holds the tabulated ids and lifetimes, the volume-to-moles conversion, the missing liquid and bulk volume, the carbonic-acid route with its saturation and pH, the chlorine hydrolysis, the dissolved/headspace split for a sparingly soluble gas, the dry vessel and the single consumption of the offer.

### Implemented example: acids and lime water from the library graph

**Four acids keep the graph the builder shows.** `Salpetersäure`, `Benzoesäure`, `Sorbinsäure` and `Tetradecansäure (Myristinsäure)` were the last reference-only acids of the catalog. The obstacle was the spelling, not the chemistry: the hand-written acid-base table in `StructureManager` keys its families on its own SMILES strings, and the library draws the same molecule with another atom order and its own charges, so a graph from the library would have found no entry. Instead of adding a second graph per acid, the catalog entry carries `aqueousAcidBase` on its `MoleculeDefinition`, the route the custom-compound path already takes. `libraryAcidBase()` attaches the data to the library graph, `acidReagent()` (then still named `solidAcid()`) builds a weighed portion from it and `acidSolution()` a stock, so the palette card, the dose, the conjugate-base cards and the structure in the builder stay one graph and no SMILES spelling is repeated.

**Nitric acid** is modelled as the strong acid it is, pKa −1.3, with one proton on an oxygen. Only that role exists: it does not oxidise copper, iron(II) or organic material, the same documented gap the permanganate and the thiosulfate carry as spectator ions. The stock takes the 65 % reagent of 1.39 g/mL, which is 0.65 · 1390 / 63.012 = 14.34 mol/L and stands as 14.4 beside the other concentrated reagents; 0.1 mol/L reads pH 1.002 and 0.01 mol/L pH 2.000, one unit apart.

**The three carboxylic acids** are weighed solids, and their density only sets the bulk volume the portion occupies before it dissolves: the model has no solubility product for a molecular crystal, so the whole portion dissolves and the acid equilibrium alone sets the pH. pKa 4.19 for benzoic acid, 4.76 for sorbic acid and 4.9 for myristic acid, whose apparent constant spreads over 4.5–5.3 with the method. A 5 mmol/L portion reads pH 3.270, 3.543 and 3.611 against 3.2455, 3.5305 and 3.6005 from the closed approximation pH = (pKa − log c)/2; the difference is the approximation, which drops the water term, plus the bulk volume. Benzoic acid at 0.5 mol/L, eighteen times its real solubility of about 0.028 mol/L, still gives the undissolved-free answer within 0.05 pH units. That limit is a real gap of the model, not a dosing error, and it does not exempt the reagent: it doses, dissolves and neutralises like its tabulated siblings.

**Slaked lime** is the first hydroxide with both forms. `Calciumhydroxid` is the weighed solid and `Kalkwasser` the saturated stock, both built from one definition and from the `Ca(OH)2` row of `chemlib/saltSolubility.ts` (Ksp 6.6 · 10⁻⁶, 2.21 g/mL). That row now also carries a `saturation` of 0.011 mol/L: the ideal solubility is (6.6 · 10⁻⁶ / 4)^⅓ = 0.01183, rounded down to two digits so a stock dosed at its own limit stays clear and below its ion product, the same rule the four sparingly soluble salts follow. Real lime water holds about 0.023 mol/L because calcium hydroxide forms a CaOH⁺ ion pair, which the model does not carry; `saturation` is a rounded selector limit, not a measurement. Measured on the pair:

- 100 mL of the 0.011 mol/L stock read pH 12.267 with 1.1 mmol Ca²⁺ and no sediment. A weighed 1.1 mmol in 100 mL water reaches the same bath to the digit, because both dose paths share the definition.
- 0.01 mol in 100 mL keeps a reservoir: 8.38 mmol settle, 1.617 mmol stay dissolved, 0.0162 mol/L at pH 12.414, and the sum is the dose. The dissolved amount lies above the ideal root because Ca²⁺ hydrolyses (pKa 12.6) while the free ion product stays at the Ksp — the rule the iron(II) sulfide row already follows.
- A stock dosed above the palette limit precipitates instead of staying supersaturated: 0.02 mol/L falls to the same 0.0162 mol/L and deposits the difference, so the selector cap is not a wall.
- 2.2 mmol of hydrochloric acid neutralise 100 mL of lime water to pH 6.948 and 1.1 mmol Ca²⁺; ten times the acid leaves 0.01 mol/L Ca²⁺ at pH 1.149; 1 mmol of benzoic acid takes one of the two millimoles of hydroxide, so the bath stays basic at pH 11.992 and the calcium benzoate stays dissolved.

**The spelling of a hydroxide is chemistry, not detail.** The catalog ships calcium hydroxide with one `ionic` bond per hydroxide, which is what `saltFragments` splits into Ca²⁺ and two [OH⁻]. A hand-built, imported or builder-derived graph carries an `acidbase` bond per hydroxide instead, and that spelling never reaches the hydroxide equilibria: the acid-base split hands the hydrogens to water and leaves the neutral `O[Ca]O` molecule, which is a molecular solid without a solubility product and does not raise the pH. Measured: 1.1 mmol of the acid-base graph in 100 mL water reads pH 7.0000 with 1.1 mmol `O[Ca]O` and no Ca²⁺, while the same amount of the ionic graph reads pH 12.267. `Simulation` therefore gained no second split; converting builder-made hydroxides to the ionic spelling belongs to the manual-edit pipeline of the [planned extension](#planned-extension-to-all-selectable-compounds), where an edited structure is re-identified instead of being trusted by its name.

**Aqueous preview.** `aqueousAcidBaseSpecies` knew only formula branches (HCl, NH₃, H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄ and the carboxylic acids). A library acid brings its own data rather than its formula, so the function now also takes the single proton that sits on an oxygen by a single bond and offers hydronium, the conjugate base and the parent molecule: nitric acid to nitrate. A polyprotic library acid stays without a picture until the steps can be drawn one after another.

`tests/acidbase-reagents.test.ts` checks the four graphs against `structureGraphs` and against `catalogStructures`, their molar masses and acid-base data, the phase, density and concentration-limit rules, the lime fragments `[Ca2+]` and two `[OH-]` from one formula unit, dry/stock parity at 1.1 mmol, the reservoir of an overdose with its conserved moles, the precipitating stock, the four neutralisations, the complete dissolution of the solids with one solute left and no sediment, and the two hydroxide spellings. `tests/aqueous-preview.test.ts` holds the nitric-acid preview beside the sulfuric-acid one.

### Implemented example: the six oxides

**The six catalog oxides are weighed solids.** `Kupfer(II)-oxid`, `Magnesiumoxid`, `Calciumoxid`, `Eisen(III)-oxid`, `Mangan(IV)-oxid` and `Siliciumdioxid` were the last reference-only entries of the inorganic block. They are molecular crystals with a covalent graph, so neither existing route could take them: `StructureManager.isSalt` looks for an ionic bond, and the acid-base table needs an `acidbase` bond whose SMILES it knows, while CuO, MgO, CaO, Fe₂O₃, MnO₂ and SiO₂ are drawn with ordinary covalent bonds. `chemlib/oxides.ts` therefore identifies an oxide from the atoms alone: exactly one element besides oxygen, no hydrogen, the exact stoichiometry and nothing else — bond orders and formal charges do not enter, because the builder's ionic charge transfer rewrites them and a dosed oxide must not depend on that. The table keys the row by the element symbol.

**The attack is a term list.** `OxideAttack` names the cation, how many cations and protons one formula unit consumes and how much water it releases, and `Simulation.sOxideAcid` runs it with the titratable acidity `sMetalAcid` already uses — the free protons plus the protons the acids still hold above pH 7, but no cation acids. A weak acid therefore delivers what it holds and an Fe³⁺ solution nothing, and quicklime does not fizz in vinegar that has already been spent. It books the cation and nothing else: the protons it takes are exactly the ones that balanced that cation, so booking them again as `excessCharge`, the way a half-reaction books H⁺, would leave the acid protonated and a lime suspension standing at pH 1. The released water goes through `addMolecule` on the solvent so that the solution volume and every concentration follow.

- CuO takes two protons: 1 mmol in 3 mmol HCl of 100 mL reads pH 2.0130 with 1.000 mmol Cu²⁺ and no sediment, because the leftover 1 mmol of acid sets the pH. In water alone the portion survives at pH 7.0000 and 79.5 mg of sediment.
- MgO takes two protons as well: 1 mmol with exactly 2 mmol HCl returns to pH 6.655, the neutral bath the consumed acid leaves behind.
- Fe₂O₃ takes six protons per formula unit and gives two cations. Half a millimole in 6 mmol HCl reads pH 1.523 with 1.000 mmol Fe³⁺ and no Fe(OH)₃, so the attack runs ahead of the hydroxide equilibria instead of fighting them.
- MnO₂ and SiO₂ carry a `note` instead of an attack and say why: manganese dioxide needs the permanganate couple, which the model does not have, and silica is an acid that only hydrofluoric acid opens. Both stay in the vessel unchanged — 1 mmol MnO₂ in 3 mmol HCl keeps its 86.9 mg — and no cation is booked, so the note is not a silent stand-in for chemistry.

**Slaking runs after the attack, not before.** Quicklime is the one oxide that also reacts with water, `CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂`, and the fresh hydroxide takes the place of the oxide in the sediment while the solvent loses its water. It exists so that a pinch of quicklime is caustic in a beaker of water: 2 mmol in 100 mL read pH 12.414 with 1.617 mmol dissolved calcium, 0.383 mmol hydroxide body and 28.4 mg of sediment, the same bath the `Calciumhydroxid` catalog entry produces. The order of the two reactions is what the lime-water pair measures: where an acid can take the whole portion, `CaO + 2 H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O` is the shorter way to the net reaction of slaking and neutralising, and 1.1 mmol quicklime in 2.2 mmol HCl reads pH 6.948 with 1.1 mmol Ca²⁺ and no body — before the acid, after the acid, and with `Calciumhydroxid` dosed in its place, all four to the digit. Slaking first used to read pH 8.517 in that beaker, because the freshly made hydroxide reached `equilibrateHydroxides` inside an acid bath. With an excess of acid, 1.1 mmol quicklime in 3 mmol HCl reads pH 2.110 from the leftover 0.8 mmol.

### Implemented example: sodium and potassium

**Two reference rows became weighed metal pieces.** `Natrium` and `Kalium` were the last two entries of the metal block without a dose: `reference("Natrium", "Metalle", "Na")` names a substance but carries no recipe, so a handoff could only store the structure. They now read `element("Natrium", "Na", "sodium", 0.968)` and `element("Kalium", "K", "potassium", 0.862)`, the same constructor that already doses iron, copper, zinc, magnesium, calcium and the noble metals. The density is the bulk density of a piece, so it only sets the volume the portion occupies in the vessel; nothing about it is a solubility. `Calcium` was already dosed this way and was simply inert in water before this step, because no solvation path looked at a metal that sits below the hydrogen couple without an acid.

**A metal that decomposes water.** `chemlib/halfReactions.ts` already listed the metals that cannot be deposited from an aqueous solution (`waterReactiveSymbols`: Li, K, Cs, Ba, Ca, Na) and `reactsWithWater` reads that list for a deposit. `Simulation.sMetalWater` now uses the same list for a dosed piece: it picks the solids whose element is on it and whose oxidation couple the table knows, takes `min(available metal, water in the solvent)`, removes the metal, stores the cation of the couple, releases the bulk volume over `metalBulkLitresPerMol` and admits `H₂` at one half per transferred electron, `2 M + 2 H₂O → 2 M⁺ + 2 OH⁻ + H₂`. The solvent counts as inexhaustible up to the water the vessel holds, which only bounds a very small portion; 1 mmol of sodium in 1 mL of water therefore reaches the solver's bound at pH 14.0000. The gas goes through `admitGas`, so it is dissolved first and escapes above its saturation like every other gas of the model.

**The hydroxide is not booked separately.** A stored cation is a spectator ion of the charge balance (`spectatorCharge`), so with no anion to pair it the solver has to leave the water's own hydroxide in solution. Adding the same amount to `excessCharge`, the way `applyHalfReaction` does for a water-reactive deposit, counts the base twice, because that branch skips the cation term: 2 mmol of sodium in 100 mL read pH 12.602 with 0.040 mol/L hydroxide instead of pH 12.301 with 0.020 mol/L. The piece is consumed at once rather than over the simulation steps, because the metal has no wetted area of its own; a portion seen over several steps would need the surface model the electrodes use.

**The order to the displacement matters.** `sMetalWater` runs after `sMetalAcid` and before `sRedoxSolution`. The acid comes first because it is the shorter way to the same hydrogen: the metal takes the protons the acid holds and pairs its cation with the acid's anion. The water comes before the displacement because a metal that attacks the solvent never reaches a dissolved cation as the elemental metal: 2 mmol of sodium in 100 mL of 0.01 mol/L copper sulfate leaves 2.000 mmol Na⁺, no copper plating and 97.6 mg of Cu(OH)₂ at pH 7.757, the hydroxide it made precipitating the copper, whereas zinc in the same bath still deposits 63.47 mg of copper at pH 6.392.

- Sodium, 2 mmol in 100 mL of water: pH 12.3010, 2.000 mmol Na⁺, 1.000 mmol H₂ (78 µmol dissolved, 922 µmol escaped), no sediment and no metal left.
- Potassium, 1 mmol in 100 mL: pH 12.0000, 1.000 mmol K⁺, 0.500 mmol H₂.
- Calcium, 1 mmol in 100 mL: pH 12.2310, 1.000 mmol Ca²⁺, 0.500 mmol H₂ and a clear lime water, because 0.01 mol/L calcium with twice its hydroxide stays below the solubility product; the reading sits a little under the ideal 12.30 only because of the hydrolysis of the cation.
- Zinc, 2 mmol in 100 mL: pH 7.0000, no hydrogen, all 130.8 mg still in the vessel. The list of water-reactive elements is the only gate, so a metal outside it stays whole.
- Sodium, 2 mmol in 3 mmol HCl of 100 mL: pH 2.0130 from the 1 mmol of acid the metal leaves, 1.000 mmol H₂, no hydroxide. In 1 mmol of acid the acid takes one millimole and the water the second: pH 11.9960 with the same 1.000 mmol of hydrogen, so the two paths add up to one half mole per atom either way.
- Sodium, 2 mmol in 1 mmol of lye of 100 mL: pH 12.4730 with 3 mmol of hydroxide in 101 mL, so an alkaline solution does not stop the attack.
- Calcium, 1 mmol in 100 mL of copper sulfate: no copper plating, 1 mmol of Cu(OH)₂ plus the gypsum the lime does not hold in solution, 138.4 mg together at pH 7.7270. The copper sulfate solution is weakly acidic on its own (pH 4.898), so a sliver of the calcium takes the proton path and the hydrogen reads 0.5006 mmol instead of 0.5000 mmol.

`tests/alkali-metal.test.ts` checks the two catalog rows against the table, the three water-reactive metals of the catalog as the exact set, sodium, potassium and calcium in water, zinc as the counter-example, both acid cases, the lye, both copper sulfate cases and the hydrogen balance across dissolved and escaped gas.

### Implemented example: the organic rows of the library

**Twenty-four entries that had only a structure now carry a portion.** Four
indicators (phenolphthalein, bromothymol blue, methyl orange, indigo), the
amino acid glycine, two sugars, two polysaccharides, stearic and oleic acid,
sodium stearate and sodium dodecyl sulfate, seven plastics, the two monomers
styrene and methyl methacrylate, formaldehyde and bromine water were dosed as
the phase they come in. `libraryMolecule(key, formula)` returns the archived
graph with the entry's formula, `molecular(...)` turns it into a weighed or
poured reagent, `graphSolution(...)` does the same for a stock whose single
solute is that graph, and `acidReagent(...)` — the earlier `solidAcid` — adds
acid-base data to a library acid, so the card, the dose, the conjugate cards
and the builder keep one graph.

- **Phase and measure.** Twenty of the new rows are weighed solids with the
  density a supplier states for the powder; styrene (0.909), methyl
  methacrylate (0.94) and oleic acid (0.895) are poured liquids; 100 mL of
  formaldehyde gas book 4.09 mmol of the dissolved aldehyde.
- **Spectators stay neutral.** Phenolphthalein, bromothymol blue, indigo, both
  sugars, the polysaccharides, the plastics and the monomers read pH 7.000 in
  water, because the catalog keeps them as spectator molecules: the model has
  no acidity, no indicator colour change, no hydrolysis and no polymer
  degradation. Sodium stearate, sodium dodecyl sulfate and methyl orange carry
  an internal ±1 that sums to zero, so they are spectators too and stay
  neutral; only a net charge would enter `spectatorCharge`.
- **Glycine is the one ampholyte.** pKa 2.34 and 9.58, isoelectric at
  pH 6.041 for 1 mmol in 100 mL. Half an equivalent of acid and of lye moves
  the bath less than the same amount without the amino acid, in both
  directions.
- **The fatty acids dissociate.** Stearic acid (pKa 4.9, 1 mmol in 100 mL)
  reads pH 3.458 and oleic acid (pKa 5.02, 1 mL = 3.17 mmol) pH 3.263. The
  model dissolves the whole portion because a molecular crystal has no
  solubility product; the reading is the acid equilibrium, not a saturation.
- **A weighed portion stays a body.** `solidSediment` counts dosed molecular
  solids, so 1 mmol of a dye, a sugar or a plastic keeps its mass in the vessel
  while it takes part in the equilibria. `splitLiquidPhases` had to learn the
  same distinction: a hydrocarbon graph reads as a water-immiscible liquid, so
  a weighed polyethylene granule would have appeared as a floating organic
  layer. The phase split now treats a dosed solid like a settled salt, and only
  a poured liquid forms a band.
- **A poured band is as thick as its portion.** `Molecule.liquidBulkLitresPerMol`
  carries the molar volume of a dosed liquid from its own recipe, so 1 mL of
  styrene gives a 1.0 mL organic layer of 0.909 g/mL that floats on the water;
  the phase split used to fall back to a typical hydrocarbon density and drew
  1.3 mL. Methyl methacrylate stays miscible, because the ester oxygen makes it
  polar to the classifier.
- **Bromine water is a saturated stock.** `Br₂ (aq)` as a single solute with
  its concentration limit at the tabulated Henry solubility of bromine,
  0.21 mol/L. The halogen/halide couple belongs to the electrodes, so a dose is
  a spectator and reads pH 7.000.
- **Formaldehyde enters as a solute.** `gasReagent` accepts a row without a
  `gas` key when `parts` names what the dose books: there is no Henry row for
  formaldehyde, and a portion that escaped again would be booked twice.
- **One library gap shows through.** Starch and cellulose share the same
  disaccharide cut in the archive, so their graphs are identical in atoms,
  bonds and molar mass.

`tests/organic-reagents.test.ts` checks the 24 rows against their phase,
density or stock rule, the bromine solubility limit, the formaldehyde gas
volume, glycine's isoelectric point and its buffering on both sides, both fatty
acids with their portion amounts, the neutral spectators, the plastics as a
body without a second liquid phase, the saturated sediment of a weighed dye or
sugar, the shared starch/cellulose graph, and the styrene layer with its own
density beside the miscible monomer.

### Planned extension to all selectable compounds

Implement this as a shared identification, enrichment and admission pipeline for the catalog and manually edited compounds, not as automatic dosing from CIF coordinates:

1. Inventory every compound selectable in Molecule Builder and join it to its stable catalog identity, including molecular structures and every listed crystal. Include hydrates, network solids and partially occupied structures; audit selectable elemental and representative-material entries alongside compounds. Record supported phases and specific gaps such as missing chemical data, recipes or simulation models. Reference-only behaviour is an interim gap, not the completion criterion for a selectable compound.
2. Maintain a separate validated analytical definition: formula-unit stoichiometry, hydrate water, complete polyatomic-ion graphs, formal charges and protonation steps. Check mass and charge balance. Preserve source omissions (especially hydrogen and occupancy) as structural provenance; do not infer missing chemistry silently from site counts, ion labels or a display formula.
3. Fetch and retain source-backed compound identity and phase-specific data during enrichment: density, solubility or Ksp with temperature and units, and relevant aqueous dissociation data. Resolve conflicting identities and measurements explicitly. PubChem molecular identity alone does not establish a crystal phase, hydrate, solubility or simulation compatibility.
4. Check compatibility with existing equilibrium, complexation, redox and gas families before admitting a dose. Reuse registered families rather than creating duplicate acid/base systems from fetched salt pKa values. Implement missing models where needed to meet the catalog-wide requirement. While unresolved, retain a specific reason and track the compound as incomplete; do not fabricate chemical behaviour to enable a dosing button.
5. For every listed or successfully identified manually altered salt, implement separate solid and aqueous recipes linked to one compound identity, including solid reservoirs, dissolution and solution concentration limits. For other compounds, supply the chemically appropriate dosing phase or phases. Label the chosen phase and keep handoff consistent with the selected representation. Preserve existing catalog identities and saved data where possible, exact structural validation and reagent permissions. Reject stale original-recipe matches for edited chemistry and route those structures through re-identification; a matching name or formula is insufficient authorization.
6. Verify each selectable compound with representation-to-reagent handoff, formula/charge/mass conservation, appropriate phase behaviour and save/restore. For salts, test both solid and solution paths, dissolution, saturation, dilution, relevant pH/precipitation/gas behaviour, and agreement between the two forms after dissolution. Include compatibility cases involving existing reagents. Maintain a coverage report of all selectable compounds and both salt forms, with remaining gaps clearly identified; completion requires direct ChemLab compatibility for the full selectable compound catalog.
7. Test manual inorganic edits end to end: fresh identification and data retrieval, invalidation of stale asynchronous lookup results, correct solid/solution recipes, measured-data provenance, solubility and coupled equilibria, reagent interactions and save/restore. Include inorganic molecules, salts with polyatomic ions, changed protonation and stoichiometry, hydrates, invalid/incomplete structures and lookup failures. Compare the custom-compound result against the corresponding validated catalog reagent where available, and verify that layout-only edits do not change chemical identity.

`tests/bicarbonate.test.ts` supplies the first regression case: unchanged and edited crystal matching, phase-specific handoff, aqueous preview, dry-salt mass and charge, dissolution and saturation reservoirs, dilution, dry and wet save/restore, pH and sodium stoichiometry, interconversion with carbonate through one acid/base equivalent, and consistent CO₂ release. The NaHCO₃ solubility entry uses the actual fragment SMILES `[O-]C(O)=O` so solid registration and aqueous speciation use the same ion identity.

Step 1 and the coverage part of step 6 run against the live modules: [ChemLab structure coverage](structure-library-coverage.md) lists every selectable compound with its dose, every salt identity with its solid recipe, aqueous stock and reference-only forms, and the remaining gaps. It is generated by `scripts/generate-structure-coverage.ts` from `data/chemicals.ts`, `molecule-builder/catalog.ts` and `chemlib/saltSolubility.ts`; `tests/structure-coverage.test.ts` fails when the tracked report or its JSON falls behind the catalog. The table joins a solid and a stock by the catalog formula without hydrate water, so a pentahydrate reference belongs to the same analytical compound as its anhydrous stock.

## Orbital view

The 3D molecule model selector offers one orbital at a time so the phase and nodal shapes stay legible. Blue and orange are opposite signs of the wavefunction. Either sign convention describes the same orbital; neither color denotes an electric charge or an electron population.

Ten catalog specimens have offline Kohn–Sham orbital surfaces computed with PBE0/def2-SVP at their displayed nuclear geometry, including H₂, O₂, N₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, ethene, ethyne, CO₂ and formaldehyde. The generator samples at 0.22 Å and draws the two signed surfaces at |ψ| = 0.045 bohr⁻³ᐟ². O₂ uses spin-polarized triplet calculations and offers both singly occupied α orbitals. Degenerate orbital shapes depend on the chosen basis within the degenerate subspace. Virtual orbital shapes depend on the finite basis set. [PySCF](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0006074) · [PBE0](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.478522) · [def2 basis](https://doi.org/10.1039/B508541A)

Other molecules and edited geometries offer a labeled local valence-bond illustration of supported σ bonds, π bonds and lone pairs. Its orientation follows the geometry, and coplanar π overlap shows two opposite phases. The fallback describes local bonding and does not calculate energies, delocalized states, transition-metal orbital populations, or unsupported coordination. Odd-electron graphs and neutral O₂ without matching reference coordinates bypass the closed-shell fallback. Two-dimensional structures, lattice views and whole-solution previews do not expose the molecule model selector. ## Regenerating the checked-in data

Use Python with ASE 3.26.0, Gemmi 0.7.3, and RDKit 2025.3.5. `data/structures/sources.json` identifies the records, molecular connectivity, hand-curated representations, and the coverage set. The generated snapshots are shipped with the widgets and require no runtime network access.

1. Put PubChem's computed conformer SDFs in `CACHE/pubchem/<CID>.sdf`. The endpoint is `/rest/pug/compound/cid/<CID>/SDF?record_type=3d`. Save a record only if the request succeeds. Missing records fall back to a local estimate.
2. Query COD with the comma-separated archive IDs from `crystals` and `metalCifs`, using `format=zip`. Save the CIF archive in `CACHE/cod-selected.zip`. [COD REST query formats](https://wiki.crystallography.net/RESTful_API/)
3. Run `python scripts/generate-structure-library.py CACHE`. Inspect the generated changes and run `tests/molecule-builder.test.ts` using the repository test command. The suite validates coverage, site coordinates and occupancies, example geometries, and dose matching.

4. With PySCF 2.11.0 and scikit-image 0.25.2, run `OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 python scripts/generate-reference-orbitals.py` after changing a reference geometry. The packed, indexed meshes retain their absolute coordinates and are decoded only when displayed.
